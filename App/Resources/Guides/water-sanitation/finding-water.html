Do this now

  1. Check your home first: stored water, water heater, pipes, ice.
  2. Look for moving water like streams and springs over still ponds. Collect it upstream of people and animals.
  3. Catch rain with tarps, buckets, or gutters into clean containers.
  4. Treat all water from outdoors before drinking (boil, chemical, or filter plus chemical).
  5. Stay out of the heat and limit hard work to lower how much water you lose.

Outdoor sources, best to worst

  1. Rain caught directly in clean containers.
  2. Springs where water comes out of the ground.
  3. Fast-moving streams and rivers.
  4. Lakes, then ponds.
  5. Puddles and stagnant water: last resort.

Where to look

  • Low ground, valley floors, and the outside bends of dry streambeds. Dig where the sand is damp. Water may seep into the hole.
  • Green plants, cattails, and willows often grow near water.
  • Animal trails heading downhill. Birds circling or flying low in the evening.
  • Morning dew: wipe it from grass with a cloth and wring it out.
  • Snow and ice: melt them first. Eating snow lowers your body temperature. Melted snow still needs treatment.
Avoid water that: smells of fuel or chemicals, has a shiny film, has dead fish or animals nearby, is downstream from farms, mines, or factories, or is flood water. Flood water is often polluted with sewage and chemicals.

Plants and stills

Solar stills and plant bags (a clear plastic bag tied around leafy branches in the sun) make only small amounts of water. They may help in a long survival situation but won't cover a full day's needs. Never use plants you can't identify, since some sap is poisonous.

You can live only about 3 days without water, less in heat. Finding and treating water comes before food.
