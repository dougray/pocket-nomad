Do this now

  1. If you are lost, stop. Stay where you are unless you are in danger or sure of the way. Rescuers search where you were last known to be.
  2. Find direction: the sun rises in the east and sets in the west. At midday in the US, the sun is to the south.
  3. For better accuracy, use the shadow-stick method below.
  4. At night, find Polaris, the North Star, using the Big Dipper.
  5. If you must travel, pick a landmark in your direction and walk to it, then pick the next one. Mark your trail.

Shadow-stick method

  1. Push a straight stick about 3 ft (1 m) long into flat, open ground.
  2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a small rock.
  3. Wait 15 to 30 minutes. Mark the new shadow tip.
  4. Draw a line from the first mark to the second. The first mark is west, the second is east. This works anywhere on Earth.
  5. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right. You are facing roughly north.

Watch method (analog watch)

  • Northern Hemisphere (US): hold the watch flat. Point the hour hand at the sun. South is halfway between the hour hand and 12. During daylight saving time, use 1 instead of 12.
  • Southern Hemisphere: point 12 at the sun. North is halfway between 12 and the hour hand.
  • No analog watch? Draw a clock face on paper with the current time and use it the same way.
  • This method is rough, especially near midday and closer to the equator.

Stars

North Star (Northern Hemisphere)

  1. Find the Big Dipper, seven bright stars shaped like a ladle.
  2. Find the two stars at the outer edge of the cup (the "pointer stars").
  3. Follow a line from the bottom pointer through the top one, about 5 times the gap between them.
  4. You reach Polaris, a medium-bright star alone in that part of the sky. It is the end of the Little Dipper's handle. Below it, at the horizon, is north.

Cassiopeia, a W-shaped group, sits on the other side of Polaris from the Big Dipper and can help when the Dipper is low.

Southern Cross (Southern Hemisphere)

Extend the long bar of the cross about 4.5 times its length. That point is roughly over south. Drop straight down to the horizon.

Orion

The three stars of Orion's belt rise almost due east and set almost due west.

Moving without GPS

  • Follow ridges for views, or follow streams downhill. They often lead to roads and towns. Watch for waterfalls and cliffs.
  • Look back often. The trail looks different in the other direction.
  • Leave markers: broken branches, rock piles, or tied cloth, so rescuers can follow and you can find your way back.
  • Move in daylight only. Most injuries happen when people push on in the dark.
Download offline maps before you go. Many phone map apps can save areas for use without signal, and the phone's GPS works in airplane mode.
