Do this now

  1. On land: use CB channel 9 (27.065 MHz) for emergencies. Try channel 19 (27.185 MHz) for truckers and drivers on highways.
  2. On or near the water: use marine VHF channel 16 (156.800 MHz). The US Coast Guard listens.
  3. Boat in life-threatening danger: if your radio has a red DSC distress button, lift the cover and hold it. Then give a Mayday call on channel 16.
  4. Speak slowly. Give your position first in case the call cuts out.
Frequencies, nets, and rules here are for the United States. Other countries use different rules. Nets and local channel plans change over time, so confirm them with local radio groups when you can.

CB radio

  • 40 channels near 27 MHz. No license needed in the US.
  • Up to 4 W on AM, or 12 W on single sideband (SSB) on radios that have it.
  • Range: usually a few miles between vehicles. Can be more with good antennas and conditions.
  • Channel 9 is reserved for emergency and traveler assistance only.
  • Channel 19 is the unofficial highway channel. Drivers share road and traffic news.
  • Many CB radios have an "ANL" or "NB" switch. Turning it on cuts engine noise.

Marine VHF radios are for use on boats. Using them from land generally needs special permission. On land, use it only for a true life-threatening emergency, such as reporting a boat in trouble that you can see.

Mayday call (life in danger)

  1. "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY."
  2. "This is [boat name, 3 times]."
  3. "MAYDAY [boat name]. My position is [latitude and longitude, or distance and direction from a landmark]."
  4. What is wrong ("sinking," "on fire," "man overboard").
  5. How many people on board, and any injuries.
  6. What help you need. A short description of the boat.
  7. "Over." Then listen. Repeat if there is no answer.

Other priority calls

  • PAN-PAN (said 3 times): urgent, but no immediate danger to life. Example: engine failure drifting toward rocks, or a medical problem.
  • SECURITE ("say-cure-ee-TAY," 3 times): safety message, like a navigation hazard.
