Do this now

  1. Pick the band first. An antenna works well on the band it was cut for and poorly on others.
  2. Get it outside and up high with a clear view. Height beats gain.
  3. Keep the cable short. Thin cable wastes signal, most of all at high frequencies.
  4. Never connect a dongle or a radio you do not mean to transmit with to an antenna that a transmitter is using.

The three rules

  • Match the antenna to the band. A 2 m antenna hears 2 m well and other bands weakly. Dual-band antennas cover two bands, usually 144 and 430 MHz.
  • Height and clear sky beat gain. Outdoors and high is the biggest win. Indoors by a window is second best.
  • Short, good cable. Thin cable (RG-174) loses a lot above 100 MHz. Use RG-58, LMR-195 or better, and keep it short.

A dual-band gooseneck antenna

A compact omnidirectional dual-band antenna on a gooseneck is a good everyday antenna for a pack or a dash. Check its bands. If they are 144 and 430 MHz:

  • Good for: APRS, the 2 m and 70 cm spectrum views, weather balloons.
  • Usable but weaker: weather radio and ships near 162 MHz.
  • Not for: weather satellites (use a V-dipole) or aircraft (use a 1090 MHz antenna).

In a car

  • Put a magnetic or NMO whip in the middle of the roof. The roof is the ground plane that makes a quarter-wave whip work. Never mount one on glass with no metal under it.
  • Run the cable through a door seal. Keep it away from power wires and do not coil the extra.
  • Put a thin plastic wrap under a magnet so it does not scratch the paint.

In a pack

  • Clip a flexible whip or a dual-band gooseneck high on the shoulder strap, upright.
  • Keep the radio or dongle in the top pocket with the antenna out and the cable short.
  • Your body blocks signal. Keep the antenna high and away from your back.
  • For a satellite pass, stop and lay the V-dipole flat in the open with its legs pointing north and south. You cannot do this while walking.
  • Carry a spare adapter (SMA to BNC) and a short extension cable.

At home

  • Use separate antennas for separate jobs. One antenna for 2 m, spectrum, weather radio and ships; one for 1090 MHz aircraft with a very short cable; one V-dipole for satellites; a long wire for HF.
  • One dongle listens to one antenna at a time. A second dongle lets two antennas work together.
  • Splitters lose signal. Use one only if you must share an antenna.
  • Ground the mast and add a lightning arrestor on any permanent outdoor antenna.
Never share an antenna between a transmitting radio and a dongle. A transmitter can burn out a dongle in a second. Pocket Nomad and the SDR box only listen. If you transmit with your own radio (this needs a licence), use a separate antenna for the dongle or an antenna switch that disconnects it while you transmit.
Keep every antenna and mast well away from power lines. Do not raise one in a storm.

Test an antenna

  1. Open the Spectrum screen (Radio, SDR) and pick a band your antenna should cover.
  2. Let the orange peak-hold line run for a minute or two.
  3. Swap the antenna or move to a new spot and watch it again.
  4. The one with higher peaks and a lower noise floor is better. Use the same dongle, gain and band each time. The values are relative, not calibrated.

If you hear nothing

  • Check the antenna is on the right connector and tight.
  • Try a lower gain if the whole spectrum shows noise.
  • Move away from computers, chargers and LED lights, which add noise.
  • If the spectrum shows peaks, the hardware works. A flat line means check the antenna, cable and dongle.
