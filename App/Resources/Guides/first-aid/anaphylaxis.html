Do this now

  1. If they have trouble breathing, swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, or feel faint after a sting, food, or medicine: use an epinephrine auto-injector now if one is available.
  2. Call 911. Say "anaphylaxis."
  3. Have them lie on their back. If breathing is hard, let them sit up. If they vomit or pass out, turn them on their side.
  4. Keep them sitting or lying down. Do not let them stand or walk, even if they feel better.
  5. If they are not better 5 to 10 minutes after the first dose and help is not there yet, give a second dose if you have one.
  6. If they stop breathing normally, start CPR.
Call 911 (or your local emergency number) as soon as you can. If you are alone with an adult, call first. If you can, put the phone on speaker so the dispatcher can coach you. This guide does not replace hands-on first aid training. Take a class from the Red Cross or American Heart Association when you can.
Epinephrine is the first treatment for anaphylaxis. Don't wait to see if symptoms get worse. Antihistamine pills (like Benadryl) and asthma inhalers do not stop anaphylaxis. If in doubt, give epinephrine.

Signs of anaphylaxis

Signs usually start within minutes to an hour. They may include:

  • Trouble breathing, wheezing, coughing, or a tight throat. Hoarse voice.
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue, face, or throat.
  • Hives (raised, itchy welts) or flushing over much of the body.
  • Vomiting, cramps, or diarrhea.
  • Dizziness, fainting, a weak fast pulse, or pale clammy skin.
  • A feeling that "something terrible is happening."

How to use an epinephrine auto-injector

  1. Take it out of its case. Check the label for the brand's steps if you have a second.
  2. Hold it in your fist with the tip pointing down. Don't put your thumb over either end.
  3. Remove the safety cap.
  4. Press the tip firmly against the outer middle thigh. It can go through clothing.
  5. Hold it in place for the time listed on the device. For many brands this is a few seconds.
  6. Remove it. Rub the spot for a few seconds if the brand says to.
  7. Note the time. Give the used injector to medical staff.
  • For a young child, hold their leg firmly so it doesn't move during the shot.
  • Never inject into the hands, feet, or buttocks.
  • Epinephrine nasal spray is also available in the US. Follow its label.
Always go to the hospital after using epinephrine. Symptoms can come back hours later, even after the person feels better.

Mild allergic reactions

A few hives or an itchy rash with no breathing trouble, swelling of the mouth or throat, or dizziness is usually mild. An over-the-counter antihistamine can help, following the label. Watch closely for signs of anaphylaxis for the next few hours.
