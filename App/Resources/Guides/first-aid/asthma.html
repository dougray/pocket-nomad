Do this now

  1. Help the person sit up straight, leaning slightly forward. Don't let them lie down.
  2. Help them use their quick-relief (rescue) inhaler, usually blue. Use a spacer if they have one. Follow their asthma action plan.
  3. Stay calm and help them take slow, steady breaths.
  4. Call 911 if breathing does not get better soon after the inhaler, or for any danger sign below.
  5. If the person may also be having an allergic reaction and has an epinephrine auto-injector, use it.
  6. If they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally, start CPR.
Call 911 (or your local emergency number) as soon as you can. If you are alone with an adult, call first. If you can, put the phone on speaker so the dispatcher can coach you. This guide does not replace hands-on first aid training. Take a class from the Red Cross or American Heart Association when you can.

Call 911 now if

  • They are too short of breath to speak in full sentences, eat, or walk.
  • Lips or fingernails turn blue or gray.
  • The skin pulls in between the ribs or at the neck with each breath.
  • The inhaler isn't helping, or they don't have one.
  • They are getting drowsy or confused.

Signs of an asthma attack

  • Coughing, wheezing (a whistling sound), or a tight chest.
  • Fast breathing and trouble breathing out.
  • Feeling scared or anxious.

Using an inhaler with a spacer

  1. Shake the inhaler. Attach it to the spacer.
  2. Have them breathe out fully, away from the spacer.
  3. Seal lips around the mouthpiece (or put the mask over nose and mouth for a child).
  4. Press the inhaler once. Have them breathe in slowly and deeply, then hold the breath for a few seconds. Or take several normal breaths through the spacer.
  5. Repeat as their action plan or the medicine label says.
Asthma attacks can be triggered by smoke, dust, mold, cold air, exercise, colds, and stress. During a disaster, keep inhalers on hand and get away from smoke and dust.
