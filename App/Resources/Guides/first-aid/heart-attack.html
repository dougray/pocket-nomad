Do this now

  1. Call 911 right away. Don't wait to see if it goes away. Don't drive yourself.
  2. Have the person stop and sit or lie in a comfortable position. Half-sitting with knees bent often helps.
  3. If they are not allergic to aspirin, have no bleeding problems, and have not been told by a doctor to avoid it, have them chew one regular-strength 325 mg aspirin, or 2 to 4 low-dose 81 mg aspirin. Chewing works faster than swallowing whole.
  4. If they have their own nitroglycerin, help them take it as their doctor told them.
  5. Loosen tight clothing. Keep them calm and still.
  6. If they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED.
Call 911 (or your local emergency number) as soon as you can. If you are alone with an adult, call first. If you can, put the phone on speaker so the dispatcher can coach you. This guide does not replace hands-on first aid training. Take a class from the Red Cross or American Heart Association when you can.

Warning signs

  • Chest pain, pressure, squeezing, or fullness. It may last more than a few minutes, or go away and come back.
  • Pain or discomfort in one or both arms, the back, neck, jaw, or stomach.
  • Shortness of breath, with or without chest pain.
  • Cold sweat, nausea, vomiting, or lightheadedness.
Women, older adults, and people with diabetes often have less obvious signs: shortness of breath, nausea, back or jaw pain, unusual tiredness, or indigestion-like discomfort, sometimes with little or no chest pain. Take these seriously.

Aspirin notes

  • Use plain aspirin only. Acetaminophen (Tylenol) and ibuprofen (Advil) do not work the same way and should not be used instead.
  • Don't give aspirin to children or teens, to someone who can't swallow, or to someone who may be having a stroke. A stroke can be caused by bleeding, and aspirin can make that worse.
  • The 911 dispatcher may tell you whether to give aspirin. Follow their advice.

Heart attack vs. cardiac arrest

  • Heart attack: blood flow to part of the heart is blocked. The person is usually awake and breathing.
  • Cardiac arrest: the heart suddenly stops pumping. The person collapses, does not respond, and is not breathing normally. This needs CPR and an AED right now.

A heart attack can turn into cardiac arrest. Stay with the person and watch them.
