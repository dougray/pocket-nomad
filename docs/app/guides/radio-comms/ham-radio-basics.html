Do this now

  1. Study for the Technician license, the entry level. The question pool is public and free to study online.
  2. Find a Volunteer Examiner (VE) exam session near you or online. Local ham clubs list them.
  3. Pass the exam (35 multiple-choice questions, 26 correct to pass). No Morse code test.
  4. Register with the FCC. Your call sign appears in the FCC database, often within days.
  5. Get a dual-band (2 m / 70 cm) radio, program your local repeaters and the simplex calling frequencies, and check in to a local net.
Frequencies, nets, and rules here are for the United States. Other countries use different rules. Nets and local channel plans change over time, so confirm them with local radio groups when you can.

What ham radio is

Amateur ("ham") radio is a licensed radio service for people who want to talk, experiment, and help in emergencies. Hams can talk across town on handheld radios, across states using repeaters, and around the world on HF bands. When phone and internet fail, hams often pass messages for hospitals, shelters, and emergency managers.

License levels

LicenseExamWhat you get
Technician35 questionsAll ham bands above 50 MHz (including 2 m and 70 cm, local and repeater use). Limited HF: Morse code on some bands and voice on part of 10 m.
General35 questionsMost HF voice privileges: long-distance talk across the country and world.
Amateur Extra50 questionsAll privileges.

Licenses last 10 years and can be renewed. The FCC charges an application fee. Check fcc.gov for the current amount. Some exam teams also charge a small fee, and some are free.

Key rules

  • Identify: say your call sign at least every 10 minutes during a conversation and at the end.
  • No business: no money-making communication.
  • No hiding meaning: no codes or encryption meant to hide what you say.
  • No music, no swearing, no false distress calls.
  • Stay within the bands and power your license allows.

Plain language

In emergency work, say what you mean in plain words. Don't use "10-codes" or slang. "I need an ambulance at the school on Main Street" beats any code. Federal emergency management guidance (NIMS) asks for plain language too.

Getting started for free

  • Study the public question pool with free practice tests online.
  • Find a local club. Many hold free classes and lend gear.
  • Listen first. A scanner or a cheap receiver lets you hear local nets and repeaters before you are licensed.
