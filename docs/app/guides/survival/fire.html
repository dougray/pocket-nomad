Do this now

  1. Pick a safe spot: bare dirt or rock, out of the wind, away from dry grass, tents, and overhanging branches.
  2. Gather everything before you light: a big handful of tinder, two big handfuls of kindling (pencil-thin sticks), and an armload of fuel (wrist-thick wood).
  3. Light the tinder. Shelter it from the wind with your body.
  4. Add kindling a little at a time, starting with the thinnest. Don't smother it.
  5. Add larger wood as the fire grows. Fire needs air between the sticks.
  6. Never leave a fire alone. Put it out with water, stir it, and feel for heat before you leave.

The fire triangle

Fire needs heat, fuel, and air. Take away any one and it goes out. Most failed fires don't have enough small dry fuel, or are packed too tight to get air.

Good tinder

  • Dry grass, pine needles, birch bark, cattail fluff, shredded inner bark.
  • Cotton balls with petroleum jelly, dryer lint, paper, char cloth, and fire starter cubes from your kit.
  • Pine resin (fatwood) burns even when damp.

Ways to light

  • Lighter or waterproof matches: the easiest. Carry two kinds.
  • Ferro rod (fire steel): scrape sparks into fine, fluffy tinder. Works when wet.
  • Battery and fine steel wool: touch both battery terminals to the steel wool. It glows and lights tinder.
  • Magnifying glass: focus sunlight to a small bright dot on dark tinder.

Wet weather

  • Look for dead branches still on trees, not on the ground. Snap test: if it snaps cleanly, it's dry enough.
  • Split wood to get to the dry inside.
  • Build on a platform of sticks if the ground is wet or snowy.
Never use fire, grills, or fuel heaters inside a tent, car, or closed room without a proper chimney. Carbon monoxide kills without warning. Never use gasoline to start a fire.
Check for fire bans. During dry, windy weather, a single spark can start a wildfire. If conditions are very dry, use a stove or skip the fire unless your life depends on it.
